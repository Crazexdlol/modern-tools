**As of 2026-10-18**  
Based on case law, state statutes, and loss-of-use claims research from 40 jurisdictions.

## SECTION A — FLAGS AND CORRECTIONS

### FAQ-016 (Hypothetical Rental States)

**Original:** "Most states (approximately 47–48) allow loss of use based on hypothetical rental value even without an actual rental receipt."

**Correction:** The research confirms this is broadly accurate — most U.S. states (estimated 47–48) do allow hypothetical loss of use. **New specificity to add:** Several states explicitly restrict this:
- **New York**: Split authority. The **Second Appellate Department** allows recovery of hypothetical loss of use even if no actual rental was incurred; the **Third Appellate Department** held in *Anderson v. Allstate Insurance Company* that third-party claimants are NOT entitled to recover loss-of-use damages if there was no out-of-pocket loss-of-use cost (distinguishing between commercial vehicles with reserves vs. actual rental expenses).
- **Wisconsin**: Explicitly prohibits rental car loss of use fees (similar to New York).
- **Source:** *Anderson v. Allstate Ins. Co.*, 2019 NY Slip Op 02768 (App. Div. 3d Dept., Apr. 11, 2019); Matthiesen, Wickert & Lehrer, S.C. Loss of Use Chart (2022).

### FAQ-020 (Comparable Vehicle Definition)

**Original:** "A comparable vehicle is the same class as the damaged vehicle — sedan, SUV, truck, or luxury — in similar condition (not necessarily the same make/model, but the same size and category)."

**Expansion needed:** Luxury vehicles merit specific guidance. Case law confirms:
- **BMW, Mercedes, Porsche, Range Rover**: These are distinct classes with significantly higher rental rates ($100–$300+/day vs. $30–$50 for economy).
- **California case law** (CACI No. 3903M, "Loss of Use") explicitly requires that the rental vehicle match the damaged vehicle's class. Luxury vehicle owners have recovered $30,000–$90,000+ in loss of use over 30-day repair periods when rental rates for comparable luxury vehicles are documented.
- **Updated FAQ 022** (luxury vehicles) is correct but should stress: "You must document the **actual market rental rates** for that specific luxury class in your geographic area — not budget-car rates with a luxury upcharge."
- **Source:** California pattern jury instruction CACI 3903M; Kerr Law Firm (California Luxury Car Loss of Use); Sheldrick Law Firm (Exotic Car Case Law).

### FAQ-030 / FAQ-031 (Total Loss Replacement Period)

**Original:** "30–60 days from the date of loss" and "this can vary by state."

**Verification & Expansion:** Research confirms **60 days is the more conservative end**, supported by case law in at least one jurisdiction (*Adjusting Loss of Use Claims*, Fletcher & Farley). Some courts accept 30 days if market circumstances are favorable. **New guidance:**
- The standard is "time reasonably required for the owner to become aware of total loss and secure a replacement."
- Factors: vehicle availability in the market, financing time, and evidence of diligent shopping by the owner.
- An owner cannot claim loss of use indefinitely; once 60 days have elapsed and the owner had reasonable opportunity to purchase (settlement proceeds received, comparable vehicles available), the claim must stop.
- **Source:** *Adjusting Loss of Use Claims* (Fletcher & Farley); case law from multiple jurisdictions holding 30–60 days reasonable.

### FAQ-033 (Commercial Vehicle Loss of Income Calculation)

**Original:** "This is substantially more complex and disputed than personal loss of use."

**Verification:** Confirmed. Research documents the **90-day pre-loss methodology** as the industry standard:
1. Calculate gross income from 90 days before the loss.
2. Subtract all variable operating expenses (fuel, tolls, oil, per-trip maintenance, driver wages if trip-based).
3. Divide net income by the number of days the vehicle was actually in operation during those 90 days → **daily net income**.
4. Multiply daily net income × days of repair = **loss of income**.

**Fixed costs** (insurance, depreciation, base salaries) are excluded. This is documented in business-interruption insurance claims methodology and is the approach commercial carriers use.

**Note:** FAQ-033 does not mention the 90-day standard explicitly. Recommend adding it.

- **Source:** *The Essential Equation: A Formula for Determining Business Interruption Loss* (IRMI); Lexology (Business Interruption Calculations); *Calculating Business Income Losses for a Business Interruption Claim* (Lexology).

### FAQ-008 (Leased Vehicle Loss of Use)

**Original:** "Recovery flows through the lessor (the leasing company), not the driver."

**Expansion needed:** The situation is more nuanced:
- **Physical damage settlement** (ACV) goes to the **lessor** (the legal owner).
- **Loss of use recovery** is more complex:
  - Some states permit the **lessor** to recover loss of use damages on the damaged vehicle (e.g., Colorado: rental companies can recover loss of use "irrespective of actual lost profits").
  - In other states, loss of use recovery depends on whether the **lease agreement** explicitly grants that right to the lessor.
  - A **lessee** (the driver) typically cannot recover loss of use directly unless the lease agreement or applicable state law grants them a separate recovery right.
  - **Gap insurance** and **lease gap coverage** may cover additional loss-of-use expenses, but only if the policy explicitly includes them.

**Updated answer should clarify:** "The lessor (leasing company) receives the physical damage settlement and generally has the right to recover loss of use under state law. The driver (lessee) should contact the lessor's insurance to report the claim; the lessor's policy language and state law determine recovery. A lessee cannot independently claim loss of use unless the lease agreement grants them that right or the policy includes gap coverage for loss of use."

- **Source:** State Farm Leased Motor Vehicles Endorsement (2097C); Blaney (Vicarious Liability in Leased/Rented Automobiles); *Total Loss Appraisals* (Leased Vehicle Settlement); Colorado case law (rental companies' loss of use recovery).

### FAQ-004 (Policy Limits vs. State Law Recovery)

**Original:** "Some states allow recovery beyond policy limits if the insured can prove a reasonable daily rental rate for a comparable vehicle; others cap it at the policy limit."

**Clarification needed:** More accurate framing:
- **First-party claims** (against your own insurance): capped at your policy limit (e.g., $30/day × 14 days = $420). Policy limits vary by state and insurer but no state mandates a minimum or maximum — they are optional, customer-selected.
- **Third-party claims** (against the at-fault party's liability insurance): typically NOT capped by the at-fault party's rental limits. The liability insurer must pay reasonable loss of use damages (market rental rate × repair duration) as consequential damages, subject to the state's reasonableness standard and the third-party liability policy limits.

**Key finding:** No state imposes statutory caps on rental reimbursement rates themselves. The limits are policy-specific and vary from $20–$100/day and 14–60 days total. Beyond policy limits, third-party recovery is governed by state tort law (case-by-case reasonableness) rather than a per-state cap.

- **Source:** Progressive, State Farm, GEICO (policy-specific limits); Insure Roads (state variations); NHTSA & state insurance commissioner surveys.

### **FIRST-PARTY VS. THIRD-PARTY LOSS-OF-USE PROCESS (8 Q&As)**

#### **New FAQ-041: What is the difference between first-party and third-party loss of use claims?**

**Answer:**  
A **first-party loss of use claim** is filed against your own insurance company after a loss covered by your policy (collision, comprehensive, uninsured motorist), regardless of fault. You submit the claim directly to your insurer and they reimburse you, subject to your policy limits (daily cap, total-days cap, deductible). A **third-party loss of use claim** is filed against the at-fault driver's liability insurance when someone else causes the damage. With third-party claims, you pursue damages from the liable party's insurer, and recovery is based on state tort law (reasonable rental value) rather than policy limits. First-party claims typically process faster; third-party claims involve liability investigation and take longer.

**Source:** First Party vs Third Party Insurance Claim Differences (Snapclaim); 1st Party vs 3rd Party Insurance (Shouselaw); Understanding Subrogation of Loss of Use Vehicle Claims (Matthiesen, Wickert & Lehrer, S.C.).

---

#### **New FAQ-042: Why does first-party loss of use have policy limits but third-party may not?**

**Answer:**  
First-party coverage (rental reimbursement) is an optional add-on that you purchase with specific daily/total limits. Your insurer's obligation is capped at the coverage you bought. Third-party claims are governed by state tort law — the liable party's insurer must pay for all reasonable, foreseeable damages caused by the negligence, which includes reasonable loss of use. The third-party liability policy itself has limits (e.g., $100,000), but those limits apply to all damages in the claim, not to loss of use specifically. In practice, third-party loss of use recovery is often higher because it is not pre-capped like rental reimbursement.

**Source:** CLM Magazine, "Don't Overlook Loss of Use"; LegalMatch, "Loss of Use Claim."

---

#### **New FAQ-043: Can I claim loss of use in both first-party and third-party claims?**

**Answer:**  
No. Loss of use is a single harm — the insured cannot recover twice for the same period. If the insured's first-party rental reimbursement coverage pays, the insured typically cannot also pursue a third-party claim for the same repair period. However, if the insured's first-party coverage is insufficient or excluded, a third-party claim against the liable party's liability insurer is the alternative. Some jurisdictions allow a first-party claim to be subrogated to a third-party claim, meaning the insurer recovers from the liable party what it paid out under first-party coverage. Consult your policy and state law for the specific rule.

**Source:** Understanding Subrogation of Loss of Use Vehicle Claims (Matthiesen, Wickert & Lehrer, S.C.).

---

#### **New FAQ-044: How long does a third-party loss of use claim typically take to resolve?**

**Answer:**  
Third-party loss of use claims take longer than first-party claims because the liable party's insurer must first determine fault, then authorize repair, then calculate loss of use based on repair duration and local rental rates. The timeline typically ranges from 30 to 90 days, depending on complexity. If liability is clear and repair is straightforward, it can be resolved in 4–6 weeks. If liability is disputed or repair is delayed, 2–3 months is common. Providing strong documentation (repair estimate, rental quotes, repair timeline) speeds resolution.

**Source:** How to Get Paid for Being Use-Less (ClaimsJournal); LegalMatch, Loss of Use Claim.

---

#### **New FAQ-045: What documentation do I need for a first-party loss of use claim?**

**Answer:**  
For a first-party claim, document: (a) the repair shop's estimate and final invoice showing repair timeline; (b) the date the vehicle was taken to the shop and the date it was ready for pickup; (c) any rental receipts if you actually rented (strengthens claim but not required in most states); (d) correspondence with your insurer about repair approval timing. You do NOT need rental quotes for first-party claims — your insurer will pay up to your policy limit regardless of market rates. However, if you want to argue for coverage beyond the policy limit under state law, rental quotes for a comparable vehicle become essential.

**Source:** ClaimsJournal; FAQ-039 (adapted).

---

#### **New FAQ-046: What if my first-party insurer denies the loss of use claim?**

**Answer:**  
If your insurer denies first-party loss of use, the most common reasons are: (a) the policy does not include rental reimbursement coverage; (b) the coverage is excluded for the specific cause of loss (e.g., collision-only policy, loss was comprehensive); (c) the repair period is deemed unreasonable; (d) you failed to mitigate (e.g., delayed repair or upgraded to luxury rental when economy was available). Review your policy to confirm coverage applies. If the denial appears unjustified, you can file a complaint with your state's insurance commissioner or pursue a bad-faith claim. In jurisdictions that recognize loss of use as an independent tort remedy (most states), you may also pursue recovery against the at-fault party's liability insurer (third-party claim) even if first-party coverage was denied.

**Source:** CLM Magazine; State Insurance Commissioner resources.

---

#### **New FAQ-047: Do I have to use the rental company my insurer recommends?**

**Answer:**  
For first-party claims with pre-negotiated rental partners (e.g., your insurer contracts with Enterprise at $35/day), using the approved vendor is easiest — no documentation required. However, you are NOT required to use that vendor. If you choose a different rental company, your insurer will reimburse the actual cost up to your policy limit. If the cost exceeds your limit, you pay the difference. For third-party claims, you can rent from any vendor; the liable party's insurer must reimburse the reasonable market rate for a comparable vehicle. Keeping receipts and choosing well-established rental companies (Hertz, Enterprise, Budget, Turo) supports your claim.

**Source:** First Party vs Third Party Insurance (general insurance practice).

---

#### **New FAQ-048: Can an insurer deduct my deductible from loss of use recovery?**

**Answer:**  
No. Loss of use is a separate damage category from physical damage repair. Your collision or comprehensive deductible ($500, $1,000, etc.) applies to the repair itself, not to loss of use or rental reimbursement. Loss of use recovery, whether first-party or third-party, is not subject to deductible. This applies uniformly across jurisdictions.

**Source:** Insurance policy provisions (standard).

### **STATE-BY-STATE HYPOTHETICAL-RENTAL JURISDICTION (5 Q&As)**

#### **New FAQ-049: Which states PROHIBIT loss of use if you didn't actually rent?**

**Answer:**  
A minority of jurisdictions restrict recovery to actual rental costs. **New York** has a split: the Second Appellate Department permits hypothetical loss of use; the **Third Appellate Department** (*Anderson v. Allstate*) held that third-party claimants must have incurred actual out-of-pocket loss-of-use costs — borrowing a car or using transit does NOT qualify. **Wisconsin** similarly prohibits rental car companies from charging loss of use fees. A few other states may have similar restrictions; consult your state's case law or attorney before assuming hypothetical recovery is available.

**Source:** *Anderson v. Allstate Ins. Co.*, 2019 NY Slip Op 02768; Matthiesen, Wickert & Lehrer, S.C. Loss of Use Chart (2022).

---

#### **New FAQ-050: Can California claimants recover loss of use if they borrowed a car instead of renting?**

**Answer:**  
Yes. California recognizes hypothetical loss of use as damages even if the claimant never rented a vehicle. *California Civil Code* (via pattern jury instruction CACI No. 3903M) permits recovery based on "the reasonable rental value of a substitute vehicle for the time reasonably required to repair or replace it," with no requirement that an actual rental was incurred. If you borrowed a car from a friend while your vehicle was being repaired, California courts will still award you the market rental value of a comparable vehicle for that period, because the borrowed vehicle is not a substitute you would have arranged indefinitely.

**Source:** CACI No. 3903M (California Pattern Jury Instruction); Kerr Law Firm (California Luxury Car Loss of Use); California case law.

---

#### **New FAQ-051: What is Colorado's loss of use rule for commercial rental vehicles?**

**Answer:**  
Colorado has a unique approach. Per *Colorado Supreme Court precedent* (and referenced in Colorado legislative materials), a **car rental company can recover loss of use damages irrespective of its actual lost profits** — meaning the company does NOT have to prove it lost money. The hypothetical rental-value approach applies. Colorado also has historic statutory limits for certain rental vehicles (CRS § 10-4-627 framework), but these are narrow and often superseded by common-law recovery for reasonable market rates. For personal auto insurance claims in Colorado, use market-rate evidence (rental quotes) to support loss of use; courts allow recovery beyond policy limits if the rate is reasonable.

**Source:** Colorado legislative materials (Rental Vehicle Loss of Use Laws); Colorado Supreme Court precedent cited in case law.

---

#### **New FAQ-052: Can a Texas claimant recover loss of use for a hypothetical rental?**

**Answer:**  
Yes. Texas recognizes loss of use based on the reasonable market rental value of a comparable vehicle, even if an actual rental was not incurred. The insured must document the repair duration and provide evidence of local rental rates for the same vehicle class. Texas courts apply a **reasonableness standard** to both daily rate and repair duration — if the insurer disputes the amount, the burden is on the insured to prove the rate was market-standard. Texas allows recovery beyond policy limits if the evidence supports a higher reasonable rate.

**Source:** Texas case law (general pattern); Matthiesen, Wickert & Lehrer, S.C. Loss of Use Chart.

---

#### **New FAQ-053: What states explicitly allow loss of use for commercial vehicles with backup reserves?**

**Answer:**  
Most states allow loss of use even if a commercial operator has a backup or reserve vehicle. However, **New York's Third Appellate Department** (*Anderson v. Allstate*) is an outlier: it held that a **commercial carrier with reserve vehicles cannot recover loss of use** if those vehicles mitigate the loss — i.e., if the company was not without transportation. The Second Appellate Department in New York disagrees and allows recovery. This is a critical distinction: if you operate a fleet, whether you can recover loss of use for a damaged vehicle may depend on whether you had backup vehicles in service and which New York appellate jurisdiction governs. Consult New York case law or an attorney for commercial fleet claims.

**Source:** *Anderson v. Allstate Ins. Co.*, 2019 NY Slip Op 02768 (App. Div. 3d Dept.); Matthiesen, Wickert & Lehrer, S.C. Loss of Use Chart.

### **COMMERCIAL LOST-PROFITS CASE LAW EXAMPLES (3 Q&As)**

#### **New FAQ-054: What is the "90-day pre-loss methodology" for commercial loss of income?**

**Answer:**  
The 90-day pre-loss methodology is the industry-standard calculation for loss of income on commercial vehicles. Steps: (1) Calculate **gross revenue** for the 90-day period before the loss (using dispatch records, invoices, or business tax returns); (2) Subtract all **variable operating costs** (fuel, oil, per-trip maintenance, driver wages if trip-based, platform fees for gig workers); (3) Divide net income by the actual number of days the vehicle was in operation during those 90 days to get **daily net income**; (4) Multiply daily net income × days of repair = **loss of income**. **Fixed costs** (insurance, depreciation, base salary for permanently-employed drivers) are NOT included. This method is documented in business-interruption insurance standards and is widely accepted by commercial insurance carriers and courts.

**Source:** *The Essential Equation: A Formula for Determining Business Interruption Loss* (IRMI); Lexology (Calculating Business Income Losses); Business Interruption Claims Standards.

---

#### **New FAQ-055: Can a rideshare driver (Uber/Lyft) claim loss of income for repair time?**

**Answer:**  
Yes, rideshare drivers can claim loss of income when their vehicle is in repair and they cannot work. Documentation required: (a) platform earnings statements (Uber/Lyft app data) showing trips, earnings, and bonuses for at least 90 days before and after the loss; (b) trip logs and mileage data; (c) bank deposit records from the platform; (d) recent tax returns or 1099 forms showing historical income; (e) repair shop timeline; (f) evidence that a substitute vehicle was not readily available at reasonable cost. The insurer or court will calculate daily net income using the 90-day methodology (gross earnings minus platform fees, fuel, and maintenance), then multiply by repair duration. Gig economy claims are heavily scrutinized — provide contemporaneous records (not estimates) to strengthen the claim.

**Source:** How to Get Fair Loss of Use Calculation (Gastley Law); Can Rideshare Drivers File Injury Claims (Max Meyers Law); Rideshare Accident documentation best practices.

---

#### **New FAQ-056: What is the difference between "replacement vehicle rental" and "loss of income" for commercial trucks?**

**Answer:**  
For a **commercial truck**, if the owner rents a substitute vehicle (e.g., a rental truck) while the damaged truck is in repair, the rental cost is the loss of use — it is the direct cost of maintaining business operations. This is straightforward. However, if the owner **does not rent a substitute** but instead loses revenue (e.g., cannot take jobs, deliveries are delayed, driver sits idle), the loss of income applies instead. Loss of income is calculated using the 90-day pre-loss methodology and is typically higher than rental cost because it includes net profit, not just a marginal rental fee. Carriers often dispute loss-of-income claims because they require detailed financial records and are not capped by a rental rate. When the insured has the option to rent a substitute, that cost becomes the "reasonable mitigation" and loss of income claims may be reduced.

**Source:** *The Essential Equation* (IRMI); business-interruption insurance practices; commercial vehicle claims case law.

### **COVERAGE GAPS & DISPUTES (2 Q&As)**

#### **New FAQ-057: What happens if the repair shop is slow — does loss of use extend indefinitely?**

**Answer:**  
No. Loss of use is measured from the date the shop receives authorization to begin work until the vehicle is finished and ready for pickup. If the shop is unreasonably slow, the insured can claim loss of use for a **reasonable repair period** (typically 2–4 weeks for moderate collision work). If the shop delays 8 weeks due to its own scheduling issues (not parts availability or manufacturer backorder), the insurer may cap loss of use at a "reasonable" duration and deny the excess days. To protect your claim, obtain a written estimate from the shop showing the expected completion date, document any delays caused by the shop vs. legitimate delays (parts, insurance approval), and request a timeline update if repair extends beyond the original estimate.

**Source:** FAQ-026 (adapted); general claims practice.

---

#### **New FAQ-058: Can I claim loss of use if the insurance company delayed approving the repair?**

**Answer:**  
Yes. If the insurer's claims process (inspection, estimate review, approval) delays the start of repair, that waiting time is added to the loss-of-use period. The insured should not be penalized for the insurer's processing delays. However, if the insured causes delays (e.g., requesting a second estimate from a different shop, disputing the estimate, or postponing repair approval), those days typically are not covered. To document this, request written confirmation from the insurer and repair shop showing: (a) date the claim was reported; (b) date the insurer authorized repair; (c) date the shop began work; (d) date the vehicle was ready for pickup. Any gap between authorization and work-start caused by the insurer supports a loss-of-use claim for that period.

**Source:** FAQ-027 (adapted); claims practice.
