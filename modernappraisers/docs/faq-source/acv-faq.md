# Actual Cash Value (ACV) — Frequently Asked Questions

1. **Q: What is Actual Cash Value (ACV)?**
A: ACV is the fair market value of a vehicle immediately before it was damaged or totaled, adjusted for its age, mileage, condition, and regional market factors. It represents what a willing buyer would pay a willing seller for that specific vehicle on the open market at that moment in time. Insurance policies define total-loss payouts in ACV terms rather than original purchase price or replacement cost.

2. **Q: What's the difference between ACV and market value?**
A: ACV and fair market value are legally equivalent concepts in auto insurance — they both mean what the vehicle would have sold for on the open market immediately before the loss. "Market value" is the everyday term; "ACV" is the insurance-specific legal term. Regional, seasonal, and economic conditions all affect both in the same way.

3. **Q: How is Actual Cash Value calculated?**
A: ACV is typically calculated by starting with a baseline price for the vehicle's make, model, year, and mileage from a database (such as Kelley Blue Book, NADA Guides, or Black Book), then adjusting up or down based on the vehicle's documented condition, accident history, service records, upgrades, and local market supply/demand. The adjustment process varies — some insurers use standardized deduction schedules; others perform detailed comparisons to similar vehicles sold locally.

4. **Q: What does the "broad evidence rule" mean?**
A: The broad evidence rule, recognized in most states, requires that ACV be determined using all relevant evidence of the vehicle's value — not just one source. This includes comparable sales in the local market, expert appraisals, vehicle history reports, service records, condition assessments, and published guides like KBB or NADA. A court or appraiser applying the rule will weigh all available evidence rather than defaulting to a single tool's output.

5. **Q: When is a vehicle declared a total loss?**
A: A vehicle is typically declared a total loss when the insurer determines that the cost to repair it exceeds 70–80% of its ACV (the threshold varies by state). Once declared a total loss, the insurer pays the claim based on ACV, and the vehicle's title is branded as "salvage" or "rebuilt" depending on state law and whether repairs are attempted.

6. **Q: Does ACV include sales tax and registration fees?**
A: That depends on state law and the insurance policy. Some states require insurers to reimburse sales tax (typically 5–10% of ACV) in total-loss settlements; others do not. Dealer doc fees and registration are rarely included. Check your policy's language and your state's insurance commissioner's guidance — if your state mandates tax reimbursement and your insurer didn't include it, you can dispute it.

7. **Q: Can I appeal or dispute my insurance company's ACV offer?**
A: Yes. If you disagree with your insurer's ACV valuation, most insurance policies include an appraisal clause that allows you to hire an independent appraiser to reassess the vehicle's value. If the independent appraisal differs significantly from the insurer's, either party can request a formal appraisal process where each side presents evidence and an umpire settles the dispute.

8. **Q: What is the appraisal clause in an auto insurance policy?**
A: The appraisal clause is a contractual mechanism for resolving ACV disputes without court litigation. It typically allows the insured or insurer to demand an appraisal if they disagree on value by a set amount (often $500 or more). Each party selects an appraiser, the two appraisers select an umpire, and the umpire's decision becomes binding — often splitting the difference if the two appraisals are far apart.

9. **Q: Do I need to hire an independent appraiser to get a fair ACV offer?**
A: Not always. If your insurer's offer matches comparable vehicle sales in your area and industry guidelines, you may not need to dispute it. However, if your vehicle had recent upgrades, low mileage, excellent maintenance records, or accident-free history, or if the insurer's offer seems significantly below market, hiring an independent appraiser can strengthen your position and sometimes recover thousands of dollars.

10. **Q: How do I find and hire an independent ACV appraiser?**
A: Look for appraisers certified by AAA (American Association of Automotive Appraisers) or members of the National Association of Independent Automobile Appraisers (NAIAA). Ask your insurer for approved appraisers in your area (many have panels). Verify credentials, ask about their fee structure, and confirm they're experienced with total-loss valuations — not just pre-purchase inspections.

11. **Q: How much does an independent ACV appraisal cost?**
A: Costs typically range from $300–$800 depending on the vehicle's value, your location, and the appraiser's fee structure. Some appraisers charge hourly rates; others charge flat fees per vehicle. If you trigger the appraisal clause in your policy, the losing party usually pays both appraisers' fees. Ask for a quote upfront.

12. **Q: How long does an ACV appraisal take?**
A: The appraisal itself (inspecting the vehicle, documenting condition, researching comparables) typically takes 1–3 hours on-site, plus 1–2 days for the appraiser to write and deliver the formal report. The full appraisal-clause process (both appraisers + umpire selection + decision) can take 4–8 weeks if the parties dispute value significantly.

13. **Q: What documents should I provide for an ACV appraisal?**
A: Bring the vehicle title, registration, maintenance records, service history, receipts for recent repairs or upgrades, accident reports, photos of the vehicle before damage, and any documentation of pre-loss condition (dealer inspection, carfax report, photos). These help the appraiser verify the vehicle's true condition and value — they're especially important if the vehicle had exceptional maintenance or low mileage.

14. **Q: How does CCC ONE calculate ACV?**
A: CCC ONE is one of the major ACV platforms used by insurers. It combines database pricing (based on actual retail sales and auction data), local market data, and condition adjustments to generate a valuation. The tool can bias toward regional wholesale or retail trends depending on how the insurer configures it. If you think CCC ONE undervalued your vehicle, request a copy of the CCC report — you can rebut its assumptions with independent comparable sales or condition documentation.

15. **Q: How does Mitchell determine ACV values?**
A: Mitchell (now part of Verizon Connect) is another major ACV platform. It pulls from its own database of historical sales, local market trends, and condition factors to generate valuations. Like CCC ONE, Mitchell can produce values 10–20% above or below peer tools depending on the region and time period studied. Understanding which tool your insurer used is important for rebutting their offer.

16. **Q: Why is my insurer's ACV offer lower than Kelley Blue Book?**
A: Several reasons: KBB pulls national averages and may not reflect your specific local market conditions (a truck is worth more in a rural state than an urban one). The insurer may have factored in unreported accidents or higher-than-average mileage from title records. The insurer may apply a condition deduction you don't agree with. KBB is one data point — use it as a reference, but check local comparable sales and title reports (Carfax, AutoCheck) to validate.

17. **Q: Should I use Kelley Blue Book (KBB) or NADA Guides to challenge my ACV offer?**
A: Both KBB and NADA are reputable broad-evidence sources recognized by courts and appraisers. KBB tends to reflect newer national retail trends; NADA includes older and regional data. Neither is binding — the insurer may cite their own CCC or Mitchell valuation instead. The strongest challenge combines multiple sources (KBB + NADA + local comparable sales + your documentation) rather than relying on any single tool.

18. **Q: What does "condition adjustment" mean in ACV?**
A: A condition adjustment is an upward or downward change to the baseline ACV based on factors like mileage, accident history, service records, interior/exterior wear, rust, mechanical condition, and recent repairs or upgrades. A well-maintained vehicle with low mileage and no accidents might receive a +5% to +15% adjustment; one with a salvage title or heavy wear might get −10% to −30%. Appraisers document condition adjustments in detail.

19. **Q: Does a vehicle's accident history affect its ACV?**
A: Yes, significantly. A vehicle with a prior accident on its title history (even if repaired professionally) typically receives a negative adjustment of 10–30% depending on severity and repair quality. An unreported accident discovered later can reduce ACV even further. This is why insurers pull vehicle history reports (Carfax, AutoCheck) — to identify accidents and adjust value accordingly.

20. **Q: Can recent upgrades increase my ACV?**
A: Sometimes, but rarely to full purchase price. A new engine, transmission, or major repair done shortly before the loss may add modest value (20–50% of the repair cost, depending on the upgrade's rarity and relevance to the buyer). Custom wheels, premium audio, or cosmetic upgrades typically add little to no value because the secondhand buyer market doesn't price them highly. Document the upgrade with receipts and photos if you think it's relevant.

21. **Q: How does mileage affect ACV?**
A: Mileage is a primary ACV driver. The baseline pricing from KBB, NADA, or Black Book assumes "average" mileage (roughly 12,000–15,000 miles per year). A vehicle with significantly lower mileage (60,000 miles for a 5-year-old car) typically receives a positive adjustment; one with high mileage (120,000+ miles) typically receives negative adjustment. The precise adjustment varies by make, model, and market, but expect roughly ±1–2% per 10,000 miles above or below average.

22. **Q: What is a "salvage title" and how does it affect ACV?**
A: A salvage title is a branded title issued by your state when a vehicle is declared a total loss and is rebuilt or sold for scrap. A salvage title vehicle is worth 40–70% less than an equivalent clean-title vehicle because buyers face legal restrictions (some states won't insure or register salvage vehicles) and financing is often unavailable. Once issued, a salvage title cannot be removed — even if the vehicle is perfectly repaired.

23. **Q: How do comparable sales affect ACV?**
A: Comparable sales (or "comps") are actual sales of similar vehicles in your local market within the past 30–90 days — same year, make, model, mileage range, and condition. These are often the strongest evidence in the broad-evidence rule. If you can document 3–5 comps that sold for notably more than your insurer's ACV offer, you have strong grounds for a dispute. Use local classified ads, dealer inventory, and auction results to build your comp list.

24. **Q: What is "agreed value" coverage?**
A: Agreed value (sometimes called "stated value") is an optional auto insurance endorsement that locks in the vehicle's value upfront, before a loss occurs. If the vehicle is totaled, the insurer pays the agreed value regardless of actual ACV at the time of loss — eliminating appraisal disputes. Agreed value is most common for classic cars, collector vehicles, or financed vehicles where the owner and insurer document the agreed value in advance.

25. **Q: What's the difference between trade-in value and ACV?**
A: Trade-in value is what a dealer will pay you for your vehicle when you buy a new one from them — typically 10–30% below retail market value because the dealer needs profit margin. ACV is the fair market value if you sold privately. A vehicle worth $15,000 ACV might have a trade-in value of $12,000. Insurance pays ACV, not trade-in value, even if a dealer's offer is lower.

26. **Q: How does the insurer know what my vehicle was worth before the accident?**
A: Insurers use multiple data sources: your vehicle's title history (age, mileage, accidents, title brands), proprietary databases (CCC, Mitchell, Audatex), public sales records, market reports, and vehicle history reports (Carfax, AutoCheck). They may also pull your own policy's declared value or any recent appraisals. If your pre-loss value is documented in your policy or with recent inspection photos, provide that to support your position.

27. **Q: Can I be forced to accept a total-loss settlement if I think my car isn't a total loss?**
A: No — the insurer makes the total-loss declaration based on repair costs relative to ACV, but you can hire a mechanic to inspect the vehicle and rebut the determination. If a professional repair estimate shows damage below the threshold, the insurer may reverse their decision. However, once declared and titled as salvage, the reversal is complex — it's better to challenge the total-loss call before the insurer issues a salvage title.

28. **Q: What happens after I receive my ACV payment?**
A: The insurer transfers the vehicle title to themselves (or their salvage agent), brands it as salvage in your state's DMV system, and sells it to a salvage yard, rebuilder, or auto auction house. You lose ownership rights. If you want to keep the vehicle (rare for truly totaled cars), you can negotiate a "keep and pay" arrangement where the insurer deducts salvage value from your settlement and you retain the car.

29. **Q: Can I challenge an ACV offer if I financed the vehicle?**
A: Yes, though your lender complicates it slightly. If the ACV is less than what you owe, you're underwater and the insurer pays the lender first — you may owe the difference out of pocket. If ACV exceeds the loan payoff, you receive the difference. Either way, you can dispute the ACV through the appraisal clause; the lender's interest doesn't prevent your right to challenge valuation.

30. **Q: What is a "diminished value" claim?**
A: Diminished value is the drop in a vehicle's fair market value caused by the accident and repair history itself — even if the car is repaired perfectly. A recovered, repaired vehicle is typically worth 5–20% less than an identical unaccidented vehicle because buyers penalize accident history. Diminished value is separate from ACV — it applies when the vehicle is repairable, not totaled. Only about a dozen states allow it; many require the at-fault party's insurer to pay.

31. **Q: How long does an insurance company have to pay an ACV claim?**
A: Most states require insurers to pay within 15–30 days of accepting the claim and receiving proof of loss. If the claim is disputed or under appraisal, payment timelines extend. Some states impose penalties (interest, bad-faith fines) if payment is unreasonably delayed. Check your state's insurance commissioner's rules and your policy's terms for specific deadlines.

32. **Q: Do I have to accept the insurer's ACV estimate before disputing it?**
A: No — you can challenge the estimate immediately by requesting a copy of the insurer's valuation report and providing your own evidence (comps, appraisals, condition documentation). You don't need to formally accept before disputing. However, the appraisal-clause process typically starts with documented disagreement, so having both your offer and the insurer's offer in writing is important.

33. **Q: What's involved in the formal appraisal process?**
A: If you and the insurer disagree by a significant amount (usually $500+), either party can invoke the appraisal clause. You hire an appraiser; the insurer hires an appraiser. If those two appraisers can't agree, they select a neutral umpire. The umpire reviews both appraisals, may examine the vehicle or evidence, and issues a binding decision — often set by policy to be the average of the two appraisals or the umpire's independent valuation. This typically costs $500–$2,000 total.

34. **Q: Who pays for the appraisal if I hire an independent appraiser?**
A: Initially, you pay the appraiser's fee (typically $300–$800). If the appraisal-clause process is triggered and the insurer's valuation is significantly lower than your appraiser's, and the formal appraisal supports your position, the insurer usually pays your appraiser's fee as part of the settlement. Check your policy's appraisal clause for the specific cost-allocation rules.

35. **Q: Who pays the umpire in an ACV dispute?**
A: Typically, the parties split the umpire's fee (often $500–$1,500). However, some policies state the losing party pays all costs. Review your policy's appraisal clause or request clarification from your insurer before triggering the formal process. In some states, courts may assess costs differently if the dispute goes to litigation.

36. **Q: Does California require sales tax reimbursement on a total-loss settlement?**
A: California does not mandate sales tax reimbursement by statute, but the insurance policy may include it. Review your policy's language or contact your insurer to confirm. Some California insurers voluntarily include sales tax as a goodwill gesture, especially for higher-value vehicles. If tax is a significant component (8–10% of ACV), clarify in writing before accepting the settlement.

37. **Q: What's Texas's approach to ACV in total-loss settlements?**
A: Texas uses the broad-evidence rule and requires insurers to disclose the valuation method used (CCC ONE, Mitchell, etc.). Texas Insurance Code § 2709.002 requires "fair market value" determination; if disputed, the appraisal clause applies. Texas does not mandate sales tax reimbursement, but policy language may provide it. Lowballing is common; challenging with comps and local sales data is worth the effort.

38. **Q: Does Georgia law require appraisers to be licensed for ACV work?**
A: Georgia does not license appraisers as a state requirement for auto ACV work (unlike some professions that require appraiser licensure). However, appraisers certified by the AAA or NAIAA hold themselves to professional standards and carry credentials. Georgia follows the broad-evidence rule; appraisal-clause disputes are common and allowed.

39. **Q: How does New York regulate ACV disputes?**
A: New York Insurance Law § 2702 requires insurers to pay "actual cash value" using the broad-evidence rule. If a dispute exceeds $500 and cannot be resolved, either party can demand appraisal. New York courts have consistently held that ACV is the full replacement cost for a like vehicle, net of depreciation — not trade-in value. If your insurer's offer seems low, requesting a detailed valuation breakdown is your first step.

40. **Q: What if my vehicle was financed and I owe more than the ACV settlement?**
A: You're "upside down" or "underwater." The ACV settlement goes to your lender first to cover the loan payoff. If the loan exceeds ACV, you owe the difference (called a "gap" or shortfall). Gap insurance, if you purchased it, covers this loss. You can still dispute the ACV, but the core issue is the gap — no amount of additional ACV recovery will help if the vehicle never had sufficient value to break even on the loan.

41. **Q: Can I request a second opinion if I disagree with an independent appraiser's report?**
A: Yes. If you hired an appraiser and disagree with their valuation, you can hire a second appraiser for another opinion — but you'll pay that fee unless it's part of the formal appraisal-clause process. Most people use the first appraisal as leverage in negotiation with the insurer before hiring a second. If the insurer demands appraisal, the process includes both appraisers and an umpire, so you don't directly hire a second independently.

42. **Q: How do I prove my vehicle's pre-loss condition for an ACV appraisal?**
A: Gather photos of the vehicle before the accident (especially exterior, interior, odometer), maintenance records and service receipts, Carfax or AutoCheck reports, recent inspection reports, receipts for upgrades or repairs, and any documentation of exceptional condition (low mileage, one-owner, clean title, dealer certifications). The more contemporaneous evidence, the stronger your position. Pre-accident photos are gold.

43. **Q: What if the appraisal-clause process reaches an impasse?**
A: If the umpire's decision is issued, it's binding — you and the insurer both accept it, no appeal. If the appraisers can't agree and won't select an umpire, or the process breaks down procedurally, you can pursue legal action (small claims for lower-value vehicles, civil court for others). Some states also allow you to request arbitration or file a complaint with the insurance commissioner. Litigation is expensive, so most disputes settle before court.

44. **Q: Should I hire an attorney to handle an ACV dispute?**
A: For high-value vehicles (worth $20,000+) or significant disputes (insurer's offer is 30%+ below your estimate), hiring an attorney can be worthwhile — they know state law, insurance regulations, and appraisal-clause procedures. For lower values, the attorney's fee ($1,500–$5,000+) may exceed your recovery; in those cases, self-help with an independent appraiser is more cost-effective. An attorney's threat letter to an insurer can also pressure settlement without litigation.

45. **Q: What if I disagree with the umpire's ACV decision in the appraisal clause?**
A: The umpire's decision is binding under the appraisal clause — you typically cannot appeal or contest it further within the appraisal process. Your only recourse is litigation in court if you can prove the umpire acted fraudulently, was biased, or violated procedural rules materially. This is rare and expensive; the appraisal clause is designed to be final to avoid court costs. Accept the umpire's decision or escalate to an attorney if you believe grounds for legal challenge exist.
