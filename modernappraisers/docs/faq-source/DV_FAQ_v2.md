**Q19 — 17c formula description is partially correct but omits critical context about its origin and limitations**

It is named after paragraph 17, subsection (c) of that court order. The formula was designed for administrative convenience in a mass settlement context, not as a market-accurate DV measurement. Its 10% maximum damage cap means it systematically underestimates DV on high-severity losses. Calling it an "industry standard" without this context is misleading to vehicle owners.
- **Correction:** "The 17c formula originated in the post-*Mabry* settlement implementation order (Superior Court of Muscogee County, Georgia, 2002). It was crafted by State Farm's actuaries as an administrative shortcut to process tens of thousands of class-action claims. The formula — named for paragraph 17, subparagraph (c) of that order — calculates DV as: (Pre-Accident Value) × (Damage Severity Factor, capped at 10%) × (Mileage Adjustment). Insurance carriers adopted it industry-wide because it is simple and produces low, predictable numbers. It is **not a neutral industry standard** and is widely criticized by independent appraisers for systematically underpaying DV. Courts in Georgia and other states have acknowledged its 'fundamental flaws' while allowing its use as a reference point. Independent appraisers typically use market-data-based methodologies that yield higher and more defensible figures."

### First-Party vs. Third-Party Claims — In Depth

---

**Q46: Why does the first-party vs. third-party distinction matter so much for DV claims?**

A: The distinction is legally fundamental, not just procedural. A first-party claim asks your own insurer to pay under a policy you purchased and whose terms control what is owed. Courts in most states have held that standard collision policies — which promise to "repair or replace" the vehicle — do not obligate the insurer to also compensate for intangible market stigma. A third-party claim, by contrast, is a tort (negligence) claim against the person who caused your loss; general negligence principles in nearly every state hold that a tortfeasor must restore the injured party to their pre-accident position, which courts have consistently interpreted to include residual diminished value. The result is that third-party DV is broadly available while first-party DV remains the exception. *Sources: Matthiesen, Wickert & Lehrer, "Diminution in Value in All 50 States" (2022); NAIC Journal of Insurance Regulation, Vol. 42, No. 5 (2023).*

---

**Q47: In which states is first-party DV unambiguously allowed?**

A: Georgia is the only state whose courts have issued a blanket judicial mandate requiring insurers to evaluate and pay first-party DV on every applicable claim — established by *State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co. v. Mabry*, 274 Ga. 498, 556 S.E.2d 114 (Ga. 2001). North Carolina provides a statutory framework under N.C. Gen. Stat. § 20-279.21(d)(1) that supports first-party claims under certain conditions. A handful of other states — including Louisiana, South Carolina, and possibly Arkansas — may allow first-party recovery in some circumstances depending on policy language and current case law, but none has issued the kind of blanket mandate Georgia has. For every state except Georgia, assume first-party DV is not available until you have confirmed it with a current policy review and legal advice. *Sources: Mabry (2001); N.C. Gen. Stat. § 20-279.21(d)(1); Matthiesen, Wickert & Lehrer (2022).*

---

**Q48: What states explicitly prohibit or most severely restrict DV claims?**

A: Nebraska is the broadest prohibition: both first-party and third-party DV claims are barred by *Chlopek v. Schmall*, 396 N.W.2d 103 (Neb. 1986), which held that full repair costs are the exclusive measure of property damage to a repairable vehicle. Michigan caps all DV recovery at $3,000 under its mini-tort statute (MCL 500.3135(3)(e)), limited to third-party small claims court actions; first-party collision policies exclude DV entirely. Florida bars first-party DV under *Siegle v. Progressive Consumers Insurance Co.* but allows third-party claims. California bars first-party DV under standard collision policies (*Ray v. Farmers Insurance Exchange*, 200 Cal. App. 3d 1411 (1988)) but allows third-party claims. *Sources: Chlopek (1986); MCL 500.3135(3)(e); Siegle v. Progressive (Fla.); Ray v. Farmers (Cal. 1988).*

---

**Q49: Can I file a first-party DV claim through my uninsured/underinsured motorist (UM/UIM) coverage if the at-fault driver had no insurance?**

A: In some states, yes — UM/UIM coverage provides a pathway to first-party DV recovery when the at-fault driver is uninsured or underinsured. Georgia courts have upheld DV claims under UM/UIM coverage. Texas allows UM/UIM-based DV claims in certain circumstances. Oregon offers optional UM/UIM endorsements that cover DV. However, many states exclude DV from UM/UIM property damage coverage, and New York and Pennsylvania specifically do not permit DV recovery under UM/UIM. Always review your UM/UIM endorsement terms; coverage language controls, not just state law. *Sources: myfairclaim.com "Diminished Value by State" (2025); autoloss.com "Texas Diminished Value"; dvcheck.com "Oregon Diminished Value."*

---

**Q50: What is the "policy exclusion" barrier to first-party DV, and how do insurers use it?**

A: Most standard auto insurance policies define the insurer's obligation as repairing or replacing the vehicle to its pre-loss condition — a physical-damage obligation. Insurers argue that diminished value is an intangible market stigma, not a "direct physical loss," and therefore falls outside this promise. Many modern policies contain an explicit diminution-of-value exclusion clause that expressly bars DV recovery. In states where courts have not overridden this language (as Georgia's courts did in *Mabry*), the exclusion is generally enforceable. Before pursuing a first-party DV claim, your policy must be reviewed for such exclusions. If one is present, your recourse is either to challenge it under your state's DV case law or to redirect to a third-party claim. *Sources: Mabry (2001); myfairclaim.com "First-Party Diminished Value" (2025).*

---

**Q51: If I live in a state that allows first-party DV, does my insurer automatically pay it?**

A: Not necessarily — even in Georgia, where the obligation is legally clear, insurers do not proactively initiate DV evaluations on every claim. You must separately request and document the DV claim. The insurer will typically apply the 17c formula (which tends to produce conservative figures) unless you present an independent appraisal supporting a higher amount. Failing to separately request DV before accepting a final settlement release means you likely waive it. Always file a written DV claim request and retain a copy before signing any settlement document. *Sources: Mabry (2001); diminishedvalueofgeorgia.com "How to File a DV Claim."*

---

**Q52: Does the fault determination affect a third-party DV claim?**

A: Yes, directly. A third-party DV claim is a tort claim premised on the other driver's negligence — if you were partially or entirely at fault, your ability to recover is limited or eliminated depending on the state's comparative fault rules. In pure comparative-fault states (California, New York, Florida, and others), your DV recovery is reduced proportionally by your percentage of fault. In contributory-negligence states (Alabama, Maryland, North Carolina, Virginia, and D.C.), any fault on your part traditionally bars recovery entirely — though this rule is contested in practice for DV. In states using modified comparative fault (majority rule), you can typically recover if you were less than 50% or 51% at fault. Fault allocation in the underlying accident directly impacts DV recovery, not just injury claims. *Sources: general comparative fault doctrine; Matthiesen, Wickert & Lehrer (2022).*

---

**Q53: Can I pursue both a first-party and third-party DV claim for the same accident?**

A: No — double recovery for the same loss is not permitted. You can recover DV once: either through your own insurer (first-party, where available) or through the at-fault driver's liability carrier (third-party), but not both. In practice, third-party claims against the at-fault driver typically yield higher recoveries because they are not constrained by policy exclusions in the way first-party claims are. If your insurer pays DV under a first-party claim, they may have subrogation rights to recoup that payment from the at-fault party. *Sources: general insurance subrogation principles; myfairclaim.com.*

---

**Q54: What happens to a DV claim if the at-fault driver has liability coverage limits that are too low?**

A: If the at-fault driver's liability policy limits are insufficient to cover both the repair costs and DV, you may not receive full DV recovery from that carrier. Options include: (1) pursuing the at-fault driver personally for the unpaid balance (costly and often impractical), (2) pursuing your own UM/UIM coverage for the gap (available in states that allow DV under UM/UIM), or (3) accepting a partial recovery. This underscores why adequate UM/UIM property damage limits matter — standard minimum limits in most states ($10,000–$25,000 for property damage) are often insufficient to cover both repair and DV on a newer vehicle. *Sources: state UM/UIM statutes; autoloss.com.*

---

**Q55: Does filing a first-party DV claim against my own insurer raise my premiums?**

A: Potentially yes. Filing any first-party claim (collision, comprehensive, or DV add-on) creates a claim record that insurers factor into renewal pricing. Not-at-fault accidents are treated more favorably in most states and by many carriers, but a claim is still a claim in the insurer's underwriting system. Third-party claims filed directly against the at-fault driver's carrier do not go through your policy and therefore typically do not trigger a rate increase on your own policy. This is an additional reason why third-party claims are often preferable to first-party claims when both are available. *Sources: general insurance underwriting principles; carinsurance.com.*

### State-by-State DV Law Nuances

---

**Q56: What are California's exact rules for diminished value claims?**

A: California allows **third-party DV claims** against at-fault drivers' liability insurers, grounded in California Civil Code § 3333 (full tort damages for all detriment caused by negligence) and CACI 3903J (the Judicial Council's 2016 jury instruction explicitly recognizing post-repair residual value loss). California does **not** allow first-party DV under standard collision policies — the controlling authority is *Ray v. Farmers Insurance Exchange*, 200 Cal. App. 3d 1411 (1988). Unlike Georgia, California has no single binding appellate case mandating DV; the right rests on the damages statute and the 2016 jury instruction revision. The statute of limitations for third-party claims is 3 years from the accident (Cal. Code Civ. Proc. § 338(c)). Documentation quality and independent appraisal are especially important in California given the absence of binding case law. *Sources: Ray v. Farmers (1988); CACI 3903J (2016); insurancelawblog.com (Aug. 2023); myfairclaim.com "California Diminished Value."*

---

**Q57: How does Florida's 2023 tort reform affect DV claims?**

A: Florida's HB 837 (signed March 2023) reduced Florida's general personal injury and property damage statute of limitations from **4 years to 2 years** for claims arising from accidents on or after March 24, 2023. DV claimants with accidents after that date now have a shorter window to file. Florida allows third-party DV claims but not first-party claims under standard collision policies — *Siegle v. Progressive* remains the controlling authority rejecting first-party DV. Florida courts treat diminished value as an element of third-party property damage (*McHale v. Farm Bureau*). The shortened limitation period makes early filing even more critical for Florida third-party claimants. *Sources: Florida HB 837 (2023); Siegle v. Progressive Consumers Ins. Co. (Fla.); myfairclaim.com.*

---

**Q58: What is North Carolina's statutory framework for first-party DV, and how does it work?**

A: North Carolina is one of the few states with a specific statute — **N.C. Gen. Stat. § 20-279.21(d)(1)** — that addresses first-party DV. The statute requires liability policies covering bodily injury and property damage to include DV compensation in certain circumstances, but the practical scope is contested: insurers frequently argue the statute applies only to liability coverage (third-party) and not to first-party collision coverage. North Carolina does not mandate a specific formula; appraisers use market-based methods. North Carolina's appraisal clause is noteworthy for allowing either party to **reject the umpire's award within 15 days** of filing — making it non-binding during that window, unlike most states where the award is immediately final. The statute of limitations is 3 years. *Sources: N.C. Gen. Stat. § 20-279.21(d)(1); rhdlegal.com "NC Appraisal Clause"; premierdv.com "North Carolina DV."*

---

**Q59: What are Virginia's specific DV rights, and is first-party recovery available?**

A: Virginia has a well-established **third-party** DV right under common law: *Averett v. Shircliff*, 237 S.E.2d 92 (Va. 1977), held that where a vehicle is damaged but repairable, the measure of damages is repair costs plus any remaining diminution in market value. Virginia Code § 46.2-1600 also addresses DV in the context of rebuilt/reconstructed vehicles. For **first-party** claims, the standard collision policy exclusion was upheld in *Bickel v. Nationwide Mutual Insurance Co.*, 143 S.E.2d 903 (Va. 1965), and standard Virginia policies contain an explicit diminution-of-value exclusion. In practice, first-party DV under collision coverage is not available in Virginia. Third-party claims against the at-fault driver's carrier are the standard recovery path. Statute of limitations is 5 years. *Sources: Averett v. Shircliff (Va. 1977); Va. Code § 46.2-1600; Bickel v. Nationwide (Va. 1965).*

---

**Q60: How does Texas DV law work, including the DTPA angle?**

A: Texas recognizes **third-party DV** claims against at-fault drivers' liability insurers through general negligence principles — no single binding Texas Supreme Court case mandates DV, but the right flows from established property damage law. **First-party DV** under standard collision coverage is generally unavailable in Texas; however, UM/UIM coverage may support first-party DV recovery depending on policy language. Texas has a unique additional layer: the **Deceptive Trade Practices Act (DTPA)** allows a claimant to pursue treble damages against an insurer that acts in bad faith when handling a DV claim, with a 4-year statute of limitations under DTPA versus the standard 2-year property damage limitation. The combination of third-party DV recovery and potential DTPA exposure makes Texas insurers more willing to negotiate DV claims compared to states without similar bad-faith frameworks. *Sources: ulg.law "Diminished Value Texas" (2025); dvcheck.com "Texas Diminished Value"; Tex. Bus. & Com. Code § 17.565 (DTPA).*

---

**Q61: Are DV claims available in no-fault insurance states, and how is recovery different?**

A: Yes, but with important limitations. No-fault states — including Michigan, Florida, New York, New Jersey, Hawaii, Kentucky, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New Jersey, North Dakota, Pennsylvania, and Utah — require drivers to carry Personal Injury Protection (PIP) that covers medical expenses regardless of fault. However, **no-fault systems govern injury claims, not property damage**. DV is a property damage claim; it is filed under the at-fault driver's property damage liability coverage (third-party) in no-fault states, not under PIP. Michigan is the extreme case: it caps all DV recovery at $3,000 via mini-tort (MCL 500.3135(3)(e)). New York allows third-party DV but restricts first-party recovery under UM coverage. New Jersey allows third-party DV and also permits DV claims under UM/UIM coverage. Always distinguish the injury/PIP system from the property damage/DV system — they operate on separate coverage tracks. *Sources: MCL 500.3135(3)(e); thesheldricklawfirm.com "DV Claims in FL, NJ, and NY"; NAIC (2023).*

---

**Q62: Is diminished value recoverable in South Carolina, and what is the current state of first-party law there?**

A: South Carolina clearly allows **third-party DV** claims — the foundational case is *Newman v. Brown*, 228 S.C. 472, 90 S.E.2d 649, 652 (S.C. 1955), which established that cost of repair plus remaining diminution in value is the proper measure of property damage. South Carolina Code § 38-77-30 requires liability policies to provide property damage coverage, and courts have held DV falls within it. **First-party DV** under collision coverage is significantly more restricted — South Carolina has not issued a Georgia-style mandate requiring insurers to pay first-party DV, and standard policy exclusions are generally enforced. UM/UIM-based first-party DV is contested and policy-dependent. The statute of limitations is 3 years. *Sources: Newman v. Brown (S.C. 1955); S.C. Code § 38-77-30; ican2000-dv.com "South Carolina DV Law."*

---

**Q63: How does Michigan's mini-tort system actually work in practice for DV recovery?**

A: Under Michigan's no-fault reform (effective July 2, 2020), a vehicle owner who suffers uncompensated property damage from an at-fault driver may file a **mini-tort claim** in small claims court for up to **$3,000** (MCL 500.3135(3)(e)). This cap applies to "damages to motor vehicles to the extent that the damages are not covered by insurance." In practice, this means: (1) the $3,000 cap is the absolute ceiling for third-party property damage DV recovery in Michigan regardless of how much value the vehicle actually lost; (2) first-party collision policies do not cover DV; (3) the old $1,000 cap was raised to $3,000 under the 2019 no-fault reform. Michigan DV recovery is therefore substantially more limited than in any other state except Nebraska. Owners of luxury or newer vehicles who lose $10,000+ in DV have no mechanism to recover beyond $3,000 in Michigan. *Sources: MCL 500.3135(3)(e); michigan.gov "Auto Insurance Law Changes"; premierdv.com "Michigan DV."*

---

**Q64: What are the DV rules in New York — is third-party DV reliably available?**

A: New York allows **third-party DV** claims against at-fault drivers' liability carriers — courts have recognized that a vehicle owner should be made whole after a third party causes damage, including residual value loss after repair. The New York State Department of Financial Services has taken the position that insurers must pay documented third-party DV. However, New York is a no-fault state for injury claims, and **first-party DV under standard collision policies is not available**; UM/UIM coverage also does not cover DV under standard New York policies. Third-party recovery can be complicated by New York's pure comparative fault rules, which reduce the award by the claimant's share of fault. The statute of limitations for third-party property damage is 3 years. *Sources: jtnylaw.com "Diminished Value After a Car Accident in New York" (2026); myfairclaim.com "New York DV"; thesheldricklawfirm.com.*

---

**Q65: Pennsylvania and Illinois — what are the DV rules in these large states?**

A: **Pennsylvania** allows third-party DV claims under general negligence principles; first-party DV under collision coverage is generally unavailable because standard policies exclude it and no Pennsylvania appellate decision has overridden that exclusion. Pennsylvania is a choice no-fault state (drivers can elect limited tort), which complicates third-party recovery — under limited-tort election, you generally cannot sue for non-economic damages below a threshold, but DV is an economic property damage claim and is not subject to the limited-tort restriction. Statute of limitations for third-party: 2 years. **Illinois** allows third-party DV — the Illinois Appellate Court held in *Trailmobile v. Higgs* that a vehicle owner is entitled to recover repair costs plus remaining diminution in value. First-party DV under collision policies is generally unavailable in Illinois. The statute of limitations for third-party property damage is 5 years. *Sources: Trailmobile v. Higgs (Ill. App.); myfairclaim.com "Pennsylvania Diminished Value"; thatcarhitme.com "Illinois DV" (2026).*

**Q66: What does "prior damage" do to a DV claim — does it eliminate the claim?**

A: Prior accident history does not eliminate a DV claim, but it significantly affects how it is calculated and how vigorously the insurer will contest it. The legally correct approach is to measure DV as the difference between the vehicle's pre-accident value *in the condition it was in immediately before the current collision* (including existing history) and its post-accident repaired value. A vehicle with prior damage has a lower pre-accident baseline, so the absolute dollar amount of DV from the new accident is lower. Insurers often use prior damage as a denial argument — that argument is legally incorrect if the current accident independently reduced value; the correct result is a reduced claim, not a zero claim. However, if prior accident damage is so severe that the vehicle already had minimal resale value, DV from a subsequent accident may be negligible. The distinction matters for pre-accident valuation methodology. *Sources: diminishedvaluecarolina.com "Second Accident & Prior Damage"; autoappraisalnetwork.com "When Can You Not File a DV Claim"; diminishedvalue.net.*

**Q67: Which states have the shortest statutes of limitations for DV claims, and what are the filing traps?**

A: The most restrictive filing deadlines for DV claims are: **Louisiana** — historically 1 year (civil law "prescription," extended to 2 years for losses after July 1, 2024); **Florida** — 2 years for accidents after March 24, 2023 (reduced from 4 years by HB 837); **Texas, Michigan, Colorado, Oklahoma, and several other states** — 2 years for third-party property damage claims. Key filing traps: (1) the clock starts on the **date of the accident**, not the date repairs are completed or DV is discovered; (2) in some states, the first-party (contract) limitation runs from the date the insurer denies the claim, not the accident date — but this is insurer-and-state-dependent; (3) accepting a **final settlement release** without reserving DV rights in writing extinguishes the claim regardless of the remaining SOL period; (4) Florida's 2023 reform is retroactive to the accident date, not the repair date, catching some claimants off-guard. Always verify the current SOL for your state at the time of the accident, not at the time you decide to pursue the claim. *Sources: myfairclaim.com "Diminished Value Statute of Limitations by State" (2026); Florida HB 837 (2023); Louisiana Act 423 (2024).*
