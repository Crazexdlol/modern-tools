**Q3 — "How is Actual Cash Value calculated?"**

**Issue:** The answer implies there is a single method (baseline + adjustments). It omits that there are three recognized primary legal frameworks, which courts and appraisers apply in different contexts.

**Correction:** ACV is determined under one of three legally recognized frameworks:
1. **Cost-to-replace minus depreciation** — replacement cost of a comparable new vehicle less physical/functional depreciation; common in property insurance, less dominant in auto total-loss practice.
2. **Fair market value (FMV) / comparable sales** — what a willing buyer would pay a willing seller; most commonly applied in auto total-loss using comparable vehicle sales data.
3. **The broad evidence rule** — a hybrid requiring all relevant evidence to be weighed (FMV comps, book guides, condition assessments, prior appraisals, auction data). Recognized in most states as the overarching standard; the other two methods produce inputs into this rule.

The existing answer describes only method #2 adjusted by condition factors, which is accurate as far as it goes but is not labeled as one of three recognized approaches. The answer should acknowledge all three.

*Source: Matthiesen, Wickert & Lehrer, "ACV Determination," mwl-law.com; Wikipedia "Actual cash value"; NAIC Model Regulation 902 (1997)*

## FIRST-PARTY vs. THIRD-PARTY ACV DISTINCTIONS

---

**46. Q: What is the difference between a first-party and a third-party auto insurance claim?**

A: A first-party claim is one you file with your own insurance company under your own policy (e.g., collision, comprehensive, or uninsured motorist coverage). A third-party claim is one you file with the at-fault driver's insurance company. The distinction is fundamental: your contractual rights — including the appraisal clause — exist only with your own insurer under a first-party claim. When you file against someone else's insurer, you are a stranger to that contract and have no appraisal-clause rights.

*Source: Auto Appraise, "1st vs. 3rd Party" (autoappraise.com); Diminished Value of Georgia, "First Party v. Third Party Claims" (diminishedvalueofgeorgia.com)*

---

**47. Q: Can a third-party claimant invoke the appraisal clause to dispute ACV?**

A: No. The appraisal clause is a contractual mechanism that exists exclusively between an insured and their own insurance company. If you are filing a total-loss claim against the at-fault driver's insurer, you have no contract with that company and cannot invoke their policyholders' appraisal clause. You may still hire an independent appraiser to document your vehicle's value as negotiating leverage, but the insurer is not contractually required to participate in any appraisal process with you.

*Source: Auto Appraise, "1st vs. 3rd Party" (autoappraise.com); Second Appraisal, "Appraisal Clause Explained" (secondappraisal.com)*

---

**48. Q: What options does a third-party claimant have to dispute a low ACV offer?**

A: Without a contractual appraisal clause, third-party claimants must rely on: (1) direct negotiation with the adjuster, supported by independent comparables and a written independent appraisal; (2) a complaint to the state's Department of Insurance, which can investigate whether the insurer is complying with fair-claims-settlement regulations; (3) litigation in small claims court (for lower-value disputes) or civil court; or (4) an attorney demand letter, which can pressure settlement without full litigation. The state's unfair claims settlement regulations still apply to the third-party insurer — documenting that their valuation is unsupported by local market data is your primary tool.

*Source: Snapclaim.com, "A Step-by-Step Guide to Your Total Loss Dispute"; Auto-Praise.com, "Total Loss Auto Appraisals for Insurance Claims"*

---

**49. Q: Does paying a deductible affect whether I use a first-party or third-party claim?**

A: Yes. First-party claims (your own insurer) require you to pay your policy deductible; your insurer then pursues subrogation against the at-fault driver's insurer to recover that deductible on your behalf. Third-party claims (at-fault driver's insurer) typically involve no deductible but can take longer to resolve because you're negotiating with a carrier that has no contractual obligation to you. If the other driver is clearly at fault, many claimants prefer the third-party route to avoid the deductible; if fault is disputed, filing first-party and letting your insurer fight for subrogation is often faster.

*Source: General auto insurance principles; Car Property Damage Claim: How to File and Get Paid, 2keller.com*

---

**50. Q: Are the ACV calculation methodologies different for first-party vs. third-party claims?**

A: The underlying valuation standards (broad evidence rule, FMV comparables, condition adjustments) are the same — the same vehicle has the same market value regardless of which insurer is paying. However, the practical experience differs: first-party insurers use their contracted vendor tools (CCC ONE, Mitchell, Audatex) per their policy terms; third-party insurers doing property-damage evaluation often have more discretion and may negotiate more freely since there's no appraisal-clause backstop. NAIC Model Regulation 902 governs first-party total-loss settlements; third-party settlements are governed by the state's general fair-claims-settlement practices acts, which apply to all insurers regardless of which party they're paying.

*Source: NAIC Model Regulation 902 (1997); Auto-Praise.com, "Total Loss Appraisals for Insurance Claims"*

---

**51. Q: Does the first-party insurer's ACV determination bind the third-party insurer in a subrogation dispute?**

A: Not automatically. If your first-party insurer pays your ACV claim and then pursues subrogation against the at-fault driver's insurer, the at-fault insurer may challenge the ACV amount independently. However, a well-documented ACV appraisal from your first-party settlement — especially one supported by comparables — carries significant weight in subrogation negotiations. Courts have generally held that a first-party insurer who paid ACV in good faith establishes a reasonable basis for the subrogation claim; the at-fault insurer bears the burden of demonstrating the first-party payment was excessive.

*Source: General subrogation law principles; MWL-Law.com ACV work product*

---

**52. Q: Can a third-party insurer declare my vehicle a total loss when I disagree?**

A: A third-party insurer can declare your vehicle a total loss under their standard criteria (repair cost ≥ state threshold × ACV), but their determination is not automatically binding the way a first-party determination can be. Unlike a first-party claim — where you've contractually agreed to their adjustment processes — you have full freedom to dispute the determination, obtain an independent repair estimate, and refuse to surrender your vehicle. If the third-party insurer insists on treating the vehicle as totaled and you disagree, the dispute ultimately resolves through negotiation, your state's insurance commissioner, or litigation. Critically, the insurer cannot compel a salvage-title branding on your vehicle without your cooperation in surrendering the title.

*Source: General auto insurance principles; United Policyholders, "Total Loss / Actual Cash Value Calculator"*

---

**53. Q: Is bad faith available as a remedy against a third-party insurer for undervaluing ACV?**

A: In most states, bad faith claims are limited to first-party relationships — where the insurer owes a duty of good faith and fair dealing to its own insured under the policy contract. Third-party claimants generally cannot sue the at-fault driver's insurer directly for bad faith because they have no contractual relationship with that carrier. A few states (e.g., Alabama under its direct action statute, and some others) recognize a limited cause of action against third-party insurers for egregious conduct, but this is the exception. If you believe a third-party insurer is acting in bad faith, your primary remedies are an insurance-commissioner complaint and litigation for the underlying property-damage claim.

*Source: Insurance Bad Faith Law overview (scholarship.shu.edu); general bad faith legal principles*

---

**54. Q: Can an insurer use the appraisal clause offensively — to invoke it after making a low offer — as a bad-faith tactic?**

A: This is contested. Courts have generally held that an insurer can invoke the appraisal clause even after making a disputed offer, and that successfully completing the appraisal process does not automatically insulate the insurer from a prior bad-faith claim. The key principle: completing appraisal or paying after appraisal does not mean the insurer acted in good faith before the process began. If the insurer made an unreasonably low initial offer, sat on the claim for months, and then invoked appraisal only after litigation was threatened, a bad-faith claim may still survive the completed appraisal. Courts also require that appraisal demands be made within a reasonable time — unreasonable delay in invoking appraisal can waive the right.

*Source: propertyinsurancecoveragelaw.com, "The Insurer's Appraisal Lifeboat Sank the Bad Faith Claim"; propertyinsurancecoveragelaw.com, "Timely Payment of an Appraisal Award Does Not Mean the Claim Was Handled in Good Faith"*

---

**55. Q: What is "first-party" uninsured/underinsured motorist (UM/UIM) coverage, and how does ACV apply to it?**

A: Uninsured and underinsured motorist property-damage (UMPD) coverage is technically a first-party coverage — you file under your own policy when the at-fault driver has no insurance or insufficient coverage. ACV valuation rules for UMPD follow first-party claim principles, meaning the appraisal clause is available and the insurer's standard total-loss methodology applies. This is an important distinction for clients whose vehicles are totaled by uninsured drivers: they should treat the claim as first-party and invoke appraisal-clause rights if the ACV offer is inadequate, rather than assuming they have no recourse because the at-fault driver is uninsured.

*Source: General auto insurance principles; NAIC model regulation framework*

## STATE-BY-STATE ACV STATUTE SPECIFICS

**56. Q: How does California specifically define "total loss" and what does it require in the settlement?**

A: California uses the Total Loss Formula under Cal. Code Regs. tit. 10 § 2695.8: a vehicle is a total loss when the cost of repairs plus the salvage value equals or exceeds the vehicle's ACV. There is no fixed statutory percentage — California's threshold is entirely formula-based. The state has no fixed 75% or 80% rule; insurers' common internal thresholds of 70–80% are company policy, not law. For settlements, Cal. Code Regs. tit. 10 § 2695.8(b) mandates that the cash settlement include: (a) all applicable sales tax, (b) one-time transfer fees, and (c) the vehicle license fee for the remaining registration term. California's mandate applies to both first-party and third-party total-loss claims.

*Source: Cal. Code Regs. tit. 10 § 2695.8 (text via law.cornell.edu/regulations/california/10-CCR-2695.8); California Total Loss Law Explained, totallosstoolkit.com*

---

**57. Q: What does Florida Statute § 626.9743 require for total-loss settlements?**

A: Florida's § 626.9743 is one of the most detailed total-loss statutes in the country. It requires insurers to use one of three specific valuation methods: (1) two or more comparable vehicles available in the local market within the preceding 90 days; (2) a "recognized used-motor-vehicle industry source" (e.g., CCC, Mitchell, Audatex); or (3) two or more licensed-dealer quotes in the local market area. Any deductions from value must be itemized in writing with dollar amounts and the basis explained on request. Florida's statute explicitly mandates that total-loss payouts include ACV plus applicable sales tax plus title and tag transfer fees. Florida's total-loss threshold is 80% of ACV (fixed percentage). Any comparable vehicle used under method (1) must be in as good or better overall condition than the insured vehicle.

*Source: F.S.A. § 626.9743 (flsenate.gov/Laws/Statutes/2024/0626.9743); secondappraisal.com/states/florida; theartofadjusting.com*

---

**58. Q: What is Georgia's specific total-loss rule and what does it require in settlements?**

A: Georgia's total-loss threshold is 75% of ACV (fixed percentage) under Ga. Code §§ 33-6-34 and 33-4-6, supplemented by Ga. Comp. R. & Regs. R. 120-2-52-.06. A vehicle is a total loss when repair cost equals or exceeds 75% of pre-loss ACV. Georgia also mandates sales-tax inclusion in first-party total-loss settlements under R. 120-2-52-.06 — the insurer must include taxes and fees whether the settlement is a replacement vehicle or cash. Georgia's ACV standard follows the broad-evidence rule; insurers must disclose the valuation method used. Georgia's statute on bad-faith failure to pay (§ 33-4-6) permits recovery of the claim amount plus 50% bad-faith penalty plus attorney's fees if the insurer's refusal is in bad faith — one of the more significant bad-faith remedies available to first-party insureds.

*Source: bridgewayins.com, "Georgia Total Loss Threshold 2026"; totallosstoolkit.com, "Georgia Total Loss Law Explained"; theartofadjusting.com*

---

**59. Q: What are New York's specific total-loss and ACV requirements?**

A: New York's total-loss threshold is 75% of ACV (fixed percentage). New York regulations (11 NYCRR 216.7) govern the ACV settlement process and require: (1) that comparable vehicles used in valuations meet specific "like kind and quality" standards; (2) that any condition adjustments be supported by documentation; and (3) — critically — that applicable New York State and local sales tax be added to the ACV settlement amount. The sales tax is calculated on the pre-loss ACV before any salvage deduction. Note: title-transfer and registration fees are NOT required to be included by regulation (per DFS OGC interpretive opinions), though some insurers include them voluntarily. The appraisal clause is available on first-party claims.

*Source: 11 NYCRR 216.7; autoacv.com, "New York Total Loss Threshold & Appraisal Guide (2026)"; theartofadjusting.com sales tax chart*

---

**60. Q: What does Texas require specifically for total-loss ACV settlements?**

A: Texas uses the Total Loss Formula (TLF): a vehicle is a total loss when the cost of repairs plus salvage value equals or exceeds the ACV. The statutory trigger is 100% under the TLF, though most carriers apply internal thresholds of 70–80% of ACV. Texas is governed by Texas Transportation Code Chapter 501 (title/salvage certificates) and the Texas Insurance Code. For sales tax: Texas does not require sales-tax inclusion in a cash ACV settlement, because Texas courts have held that taxes paid to the state are not part of the seller's price and therefore not part of "fair market value." However, if the insurer elects to purchase a replacement vehicle for the insured, applicable taxes and fees must be included in that transaction. The appraisal clause is available on first-party claims; insurers must disclose the valuation methodology.

*Source: Totallosstoolkit.com, "Texas Total Loss Law Explained"; theartofadjusting.com; 1800lionlaw.com, "Texas Total Loss Threshold Guide 2026"*

**61. Q: Does Massachusetts require sales tax in total-loss settlements?**

A: No. Massachusetts is one of the few states that affirmatively does NOT require sales-tax inclusion in first-party total-loss settlements. Under 211 Mass. Code Regs. § 133.05, insurers are required to pay only the ACV of the vehicle as of the date of loss. The Massachusetts Appeals Court confirmed in Ramirez v. Commerce Insurance Co. (2017) that recovery of sales tax depends on policy language and proof of actual payment on a replacement vehicle — it is not a regulatory mandate. Massachusetts uses the Total Loss Formula (repair cost + salvage ≥ ACV = total loss); the total-loss threshold is not a fixed percentage. There is no applicable statute or regulation governing sales-tax recovery on third-party claims in Massachusetts.

*Source: 211 Mass. Code Regs. § 133.05; agencychecklists.com, "Massachusetts ACV Ruling Upholds Total Loss Valuation 2026"; theartofadjusting.com*

**62. Q: Does Colorado require sales tax in total-loss settlements?**

A: Yes, and it is one of the most comprehensive state mandates. Colorado requires insurers to pay sales tax, title fees, and other transfer fees in first-party total-loss settlements under C.R.S. § 10-4-639, and the same requirement applies to third-party total-loss claims. Colorado's total-loss threshold is 100% of ACV (a fixed percentage — Colorado requires that repair costs alone equal or exceed ACV before declaring a total loss, one of the highest thresholds in the country). Insurers may require proof of replacement purchase within a 30-day window before releasing sales-tax reimbursement, depending on policy language, but the underlying entitlement is statutory.

*Source: C.R.S. § 10-4-639; theartofadjusting.com; appraisalengine.com, "Colorado Total Loss Threshold"*

**64. Q: Which states require sales tax to be included in total-loss ACV settlements?**

A: Approximately 34 states mandate sales-tax inclusion in first-party total-loss settlements by statute or regulation. States with clear first-party AND third-party mandates include Arizona, Arkansas, Colorado, Florida, Indiana, Kansas, Maryland, New Jersey, Ohio, Tennessee, and Utah. States with first-party mandates only (third-party not addressed by statute) include Alabama, California, Georgia, Hawaii, Illinois, Nevada, New York, and Virginia among others. States that do NOT require sales tax: Massachusetts (expressly excluded by regulation), Missouri (not required by statute unless policy provides), Montana (no state sales tax; statutes only require ACV at time of loss), and South Carolina. Delaware, Oregon, and New Hampshire have no state sales tax, making the question moot.

This is a summary — specific state requirements change and policy language may expand insurer obligations. Always verify the current regulation before advising clients.

*Source: theartofadjusting.com, comprehensive state-by-state sales tax chart; mwl-law.com, "Recovery of Sales Tax After Vehicle Total Loss" (updated 2024)*

**65. Q: Does a state's "proof of replacement" requirement affect my sales tax reimbursement?**

A: Several states condition sales-tax reimbursement on the insured actually purchasing a replacement vehicle and providing proof of that purchase within a defined window (commonly 30 days). Illinois, Hawaii, and Ohio are examples where replacement-purchase proof is required before the sales-tax component is released. In states with no such condition (e.g., California, Florida, Georgia), sales tax is included in the settlement regardless of whether a replacement is purchased. If your state has a proof-of-replacement condition, preserve your purchase documentation and submit it within the insurer's stated timeframe to avoid forfeiting the sales-tax component.

*Source: theartofadjusting.com; general state regulation analysis*

## APPRAISAL CLAUSE EDGE CASES AND GAPS

---

**66. Q: What specific language in an insurance policy triggers the right to invoke the appraisal clause?**

A: Most standard auto policies include an appraisal clause in the "Conditions" section. The trigger language typically reads: "If we and you do not agree on the amount of loss, either may demand an appraisal of the loss." Some policies add a dollar minimum (e.g., "if the difference exceeds $500"). The key procedural requirement is that the demand be made in writing and within a reasonable time after disagreement is established — unreasonable delay (courts have found delays of nearly two years problematic) can waive the right. Neither party is required to agree that a dispute exists before invoking; either party may unilaterally demand the process. Review your specific policy's "Loss Payment" and "Appraisal" subsections for the exact language.

*Source: secondappraisal.com, "Appraisal Clause Explained"; propertyinsurancecoveragelaw.com, "How Long Is Too Long to Demand Appraisal?"; ticerlawfirm.com, "A Primer on Appraisal in Texas"*

---

**67. Q: Can an insurer refuse to participate in appraisal after invoking it?**

A: No. Once the appraisal clause is properly invoked by either party, participation is contractually mandatory. An insurer that refuses to participate, delays unreasonably in selecting an appraiser, or obstructs the process may be liable for breach of contract and, in some states, bad faith. Courts have consistently enforced appraisal clause invocations and issued orders compelling participation when insurers resist. The appraisal clause is a binding contractual mechanism, not an advisory one.

*Source: corbittpublicadjusting.com, "Insurance Appraisal Clause Explained: How It Works 2026"; general contract and insurance law principles*

---

**68. Q: Does the appraisal clause resolve coverage disputes, or only the amount of loss?**

A: The appraisal clause resolves only the amount of loss — not coverage. If the insurer denies coverage entirely (e.g., claims the vehicle was excluded, the loss was intentional, or the policy lapsed), the appraisal clause does not apply; that dispute goes to litigation or arbitration. Appraisal determines what the vehicle was worth and how much the covered loss amounts to; the coverage question — whether the loss is covered at all — remains a separate legal dispute. This is a critical distinction: an insurer can simultaneously participate in an appraisal to determine ACV while maintaining a coverage defense.

*Source: iaua.us, "What is Appraisal"; vosslawfirm.com, "Don't Confuse the Appraisal Process With Arbitration"*

---

**69. Q: What happens if the two appraisers cannot agree on an umpire?**

A: If the insured's appraiser and the insurer's appraiser cannot jointly select a neutral umpire within the timeframe specified in the policy (often 15–30 days), either party may petition a court to appoint an umpire. This is a standard legal remedy and courts routinely appoint umpires in such cases. The petition is typically made to a state court with jurisdiction over the dispute; the appointment process adds time (usually weeks) but does not derail the appraisal. The umpire appointment by court order has the same legal force as a consensual selection.

*Source: certifiedautoappraisers.com, "Insurance Umpire Process"; gastleylaw.com, "Appraisal Umpire Guide"*

**70. Q: What remedies are available if the insurer's ACV valuation constitutes bad faith in my state?**

A: Bad faith remedies vary significantly by state. Representative examples:
- **Georgia**: Under § 33-4-6, bad-faith failure to pay entitles the insured to the claim amount plus a 50% penalty plus attorney's fees if refusal was without reasonable grounds.
- **Texas**: Knowing bad-faith conduct under the DTPA and Insurance Code can yield treble damages plus attorney's fees.
- **Pennsylvania**: Bad faith under 42 Pa. C.S. § 8371 can yield interest at prime + 3%, punitive damages, and attorney's fees on a clear-and-convincing showing.
- **New Jersey**: Courts may award interest on the claim from the date made, punitive damages, and attorney's fees.
- **Florida**: Bad faith under § 624.155 requires a civil remedy notice (CRN) prior to suit, giving the insurer 60 days to cure; if it doesn't, punitive damages may be available.

Bad faith remedies apply to first-party relationships. Third-party claimants typically cannot pursue bad faith against an adverse insurer. Requires current legal verification for your state before advising clients.

*Source: uphelp.org, "Punitive Damages Against an Insurer for Bad-Faith Handling of a First-Party Claim"; rateauthority.org, "Insurance Bad Faith Claim 2026"*

**Additional Q&As — CCC/Mitchell/Audatex Methodology**

---

**71. Q: What is the "Projected Sold Adjustment" (PSA) applied by some insurer valuation tools, and why is it controversial?**

A: The Projected Sold Adjustment is a downward reduction applied to comparable vehicles' listed/advertised prices by valuation platforms (notably Mitchell WorkCenter) and also appears in CCC ONE reports. The theory is that buyers negotiate below asking price, so the "likely sold" price is lower than the listed price. Critics — including plaintiffs' experts in multiple class action lawsuits — argue the PSA is arbitrary, not based on verified transaction data, and produces systematically lower ACV figures. In December 2024, two class action suits filed in Kansas and Maryland alleged Progressive used Mitchell's PSA to systematically undervalue totaled vehicles by applying adjustments "contrary to appraisal standards and not based in fact." If your insurer's valuation report includes a PSA, document it and challenge it with verified comparable sale prices (not just listings) to rebut the deduction.

*Source: Repairer Driven News, "Two class action suits allege Progressive systematically lowered value of totaled vehicles," December 12, 2024; snapclaim.com, "Mitchell Total Loss Valuation"*

---

**72. Q: How does Audatex Autosource determine ACV, and what adjustments does it apply?**

A: Audatex Autosource uses a market-driven approach, pulling comparable vehicles from its database of over 7 million weekly vehicle entries — a mix of dealer inventories, dealer advertisements, phone-verified vehicles, and private-party listings within the local market area. The platform applies adjustments for mileage, condition, and equipment, and — importantly — a "typical negotiation" deduction if the data sources are advertised prices rather than verified sale prices. The typical-negotiation deduction functions similarly to Mitchell's PSA and is based on the assumption that buyers negotiate below sticker. To rebut an Autosource valuation, request the full report, identify which comparable vehicles were used, verify their availability and condition, and challenge any negotiation-deduction applied to comparables that were already priced at or below actual market.

*Source: autoclaimconsultants.com, "How to Dispute an Autosource Market-Driven Valuation"; theartofadjusting.com; cityoflodi.us adjuster sample Autosource report*

---

**73. Q: What is the California DA lawsuit against CCC and Mitchell, and what does it mean for insureds?**

A: In May 2024, the Alameda County District Attorney filed suit against USAA, Progressive, CCC Intelligent Solutions, and Mitchell International, alleging they use customized software configurations to systematically underpay total-loss settlements in violation of California's Insurance Code, Unfair Competition Law, and False Advertising Law. The complaint alleges: (1) comparable vehicles are selected using matrices designed to underpay; (2) arbitrary negative condition adjustments are applied to the loss vehicle; (3) the resulting "market value report" is misrepresented as ACV. Alleged average underpayment: $3,000–$4,000 per vehicle, with statewide totals potentially in the billions. The case is significant because it is brought by a government prosecutor (not a private plaintiff) and targets both the insurers and the software vendors — meaning the algorithms themselves may face judicial scrutiny for the first time. Additional insurers using the same platforms could be added. Insureds in California should scrutinize CCC and Mitchell reports carefully and document any condition deductions with photo and maintenance evidence.

*Source: Repairer Driven News, "California DA sues insurers, estimating system providers over alleged 'lowball' total loss payouts," May 14, 2024*

**74. Q: Is there a standard for what a "comparable vehicle" must be in an ACV valuation?**

A: Yes. NAIC Model Regulation 902 (adopted in whole or part by many states) defines a comparable vehicle as one "by the same manufacturer, same or newer year, similar body style, similar options and mileage as the insured vehicle and in as good or better overall condition." Florida's § 626.9743 adds that a comparable must be "available for inspection within a reasonable distance of the insured's residence." California requires a "comparable automobile" be in the same local market area. Any valuation that uses comparables from geographically distant markets (e.g., using a New Hampshire pickup truck price for a Louisiana insured) or that systematically excludes higher-priced local comparables can be challenged as failing the like-kind-and-quality standard. When reviewing a CCC or Mitchell report, check each comparable's listed location and verify it falls within your local market area.

*Source: NAIC Model Regulation 902 (content.naic.org); F.S.A. § 626.9743; Cal. Code Regs. tit. 10 § 2695.8*

---

**75. Q: What is the NAIC Model Regulation 902 and why does it matter?**

A: NAIC Model Regulation 902, "Personal Lines Property and Casualty Insurance Claimants, Settlements, Releases and Receipts — Model Regulation" (July 1997), establishes minimum standards for how insurers must handle first-party automobile total-loss settlements. It requires that: (1) settlements include the full ACV plus all applicable taxes and fees; (2) condition adjustments be supported by documentation with measurable market-value impact; (3) comparable vehicles meet the like-kind-and-quality standard; and (4) any deviation from standard methodology be explained in writing. Most states have adopted it in whole or substantial part into their insurance regulations. When a state's own statutes and regulations are silent on a specific ACV issue, courts and regulators often look to the NAIC model as a baseline standard of industry practice.

*Source: NAIC Model Regulation 902, content.naic.org/sites/default/files/model-law-902.pdf*
